Turbocharged Engine Build

A 2006 Subaru Legacy GT I rebuilt after the original engine failed. I pulled it, tore it down, rebuilt it stronger than stock and got it back into daily use, fixing a few of my own mistakes along the way.

Brief
Stronger and more reliable than stock under sustained boost, without chasing a number the hardware could not live with
Target
~280 to 300 whp, up from ~210 to 220 stock, confirmed feasible with the tuner before parts were bought
Status
Running and back in daily use on a base map; final tune still to come

01

Teardown

  • Diagnosed an internal failure from running symptoms and a compression test, then chose to rebuild it stronger than stock rather than return it to standard.
  • Pulled the engine with a crane and stripped it down on a stand.
  • Laid out every bolt-on in order: intake, exhaust, fuel system and harness.

02

Inspection

  • Inspected the cam cap bearing faces.
  • Laid the valvetrain out in order in a tray and numbered the buckets in the head.

03

The replacement short block

  • Sourced a second-hand short block to rebuild around.
  • Found one rod bearing broken in two and significant wear on the rest when I opened it up, so it was not in the condition it was sold as.

04

Machined and prepped

  • Had the block machined along with the heads.
  • Specced it for reliability under sustained boost: rotating assembly kept largely stock with targeted upgrades (ARP studs, upgraded bearings, rings and valve springs), and reworked cooling and oiling.
  • Repaired a stripped thread at the timing idler pulley mount, drilling and tapping it for a thread insert.
  • Planned the full parts list up front so machine-shop work and long-lead parts were ordered early rather than mid-assembly.

05

Assembly

  • Assembled the engine myself.
  • Applied too much RTV to the case halves, and unevenly, without a proper tube squeezer. Picked the squeeze-out out by hand so it couldn't clog the oil pickup. The machine shop advised the oil gallery was unlikely to block, so I didn't tear it down to reapply.
  • Brushed a thin, even layer onto the cam girdles instead.

06

Exhaust

  • Bought an aftermarket exhaust from Alibaba.
  • Coated the header and uppipe in a ceramic-based paint rather than heat wrap, which holds moisture and speeds up rust. The coating cures through the engine's heat cycles.
  • Built a leak-test rig: blocked one end of the exhaust, pressurised it with a shop vac in blower mode, and sprayed soapy water on the joints.
  • Found leaks at the flanges, which would throw off the air-fuel ratio readings and likely get worse, so swapped back to the stock unit, which still suits the power goal.

07

Supporting systems

  • Replaced the PCV system, which had deteriorated and cracked, while everything was apart.
  • Inspected the turbo, sold as rebuilt. After the short block I wasn't taking it on trust; it was clean.
  • Fabricated steel bar mounts for a small intercooler, still sufficient for the power goal, that sits behind and under the crash bar so it didn't need a certification.
  • Fitted an aftermarket electronic boost control solenoid on a custom 3D-printed bracket, with a compatible connector added to the OEM harness.

08

Running

  • Cut open the first oil filter to check for excessive metal and debris. It passed.
  • Checked the magnetic drain plug at about 600 km, on the first full synthetic oil change.
  • Running and back in daily use on a base map, with datalogging set up so the tuner can refine the map remotely off my logs. Final tune to target, plus anti-lag and flat-foot shifting calibration, still to come.

Built, running and back in daily use. Tuning to the tuner-confirmed ~280 to 300 whp target is the next step, not yet done.

Learned

  • Verify before trusting. The second-hand short block had a broken rod bearing, so I checked the turbo sold as rebuilt, and a shop vac leak test caught flange leaks in the new exhaust.
  • The right tool matters on small jobs too. Without a tube squeezer the RTV went on thick and uneven, and I spent ages picking out the squeeze-out by hand.
  • Plan the build and the parts list up front, and spend on quality where it actually matters rather than everywhere.
  • The reliability upgrades and the machine-shop precision work were worth the money. Performance that holds up over time came from there, not from chasing peak power.
